Photosynthesis is a process used by plants, algae, and some bacteria to convert light energy, usually from the sun, into chemical energy in the form of glucose (sugar) and oxygen gas. This process takes place in specialized organelles called chloroplasts within plant cells. Here's a simplified breakdown of how photosynthesis works:

1. **Light-dependent reactions**: These reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane of the chloroplasts and are powered by sunlight. The light energy is used to convert carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) into glucose, oxygen gas (O2), and high-energy molecules called ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate).

   - The light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll, a green pigment found in chloroplasts. This energy is used to split water molecules into hydrogen ions, oxygen gas, and protons.
   - The hydrogen ions and protons are then moved across the thylakoid membrane, creating an electrochemical gradient that can be used to produce ATP and NADPH.

2. **Calvin cycle (Light-independent reactions)**: These reactions occur in the stroma of the chloroplasts and use the ATP, NADPH, and CO2 produced during the light-dependent reactions to create glucose.

   - The initial step is the fixation of CO2 into an organic molecule called RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate) by an enzyme called Rubisco. This reaction produces two 3-phosphate sugars, which are then converted into glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P), a simple sugar that can be used for energy or further metabolism.
   - The G3P is then used to produce glucose and other carbohydrates through a series of reactions known as the Calvin cycle.

The overall equation for photosynthesis is:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 (glucose) + 6 O2

Photosynthesis not only provides plants with energy but also produces oxygen, which is essential for most forms of life on Earth. Additionally, photosynthesis plays a crucial role in the global carbon cycle by removing CO2 from the atmosphere and storing it as organic matter or releasing it back into the atmosphere during respiration.
