Photosynthesis is a vital process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy, usually from the sun, into chemical energy in the form of glucose (or sugar). This process takes place in specialized organelles called chloroplasts within plant cells. Here's a simplified breakdown of how photosynthesis works:

1. **Light-dependent reactions**: These reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane of the chloroplast and are powered by sunlight. They involve the conversion of water, carbon dioxide, and light energy into ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate).

   - Photophosphorylation: The energy from sunlight is used to pump hydrogen ions across the thylakoid membrane, creating a gradient. This gradient drives the synthesis of ATP.
   - Light reactions also produce NADPH through the cyclic electron transport and non-cyclic electron transport pathways.

2. **Calvin cycle (Light-independent reactions or carbon fixation)**: These reactions occur in the stroma of the chloroplast and use the ATP and NADPH produced during light-dependent reactions to convert carbon dioxide into glucose (or other organic compounds).

   - Carbon fixation: Carbon dioxide is taken up by the plant, and an enzyme called rubisco converts it into a 5-carbon compound called ribulose bisphosphate.
   - Reduction and regeneration: The 5-carbon compound splits into two 3-carbon compounds (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate), which are used to synthesize glucose or other organic molecules. Some of the ATP and NADPH are also used to regenerate ribulose bisphosphate, allowing the cycle to continue.

The overall equation for photosynthesis is:

6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 (glucose) + 6 O2

This process not only provides plants with energy but also releases oxygen into the atmosphere, which is essential for most forms of life on Earth.
