Photosynthesis is a process used by plants, algae, and some bacteria to convert light energy, usually from the sun, into chemical energy in the form of glucose (sugar) and oxygen gas. This process takes place in specialized organelles called chloroplasts within plant cells. Here's a simplified breakdown of how photosynthesis works:

1. **Light-dependent reactions**: These reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane of the chloroplasts and are powered by sunlight. The light energy is used to convert carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) into glucose, oxygen gas (O2), and high-energy molecules called ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate).

   - The light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll, a green pigment found in chloroplasts. This energy is used to split water molecules into hydrogen ions, oxygen gas, and protons.
   - The hydrogen ions and protons are then used to produce ATP and NADPH through a series of chemical reactions known as the electron transport chain.

2. **Calvin cycle (Light-independent reactions)**: These reactions occur in the stroma of the chloroplasts and use the ATP, NADPH, and CO2 produced during the light-dependent reactions to synthesize glucose.

   - The initial step involves the fixation of CO2 into an organic compound called RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate) by an enzyme called Rubisco (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase).
   - This reaction produces two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate, which are then converted into other sugars such as glucose and other organic compounds.

The overall equation for photosynthesis is:

6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 (glucose) + 6 O2

Photosynthesis not only provides plants with the energy they need to grow and reproduce, but it also plays a crucial role in maintaining oxygen levels in our atmosphere.
